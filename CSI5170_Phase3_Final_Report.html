Adversarial Robustness of Convolutional Neural Networks: A Comparative Study of Gaussian Noise and Adversarial Attacks, Defense Strategies, and Explainability with Grad-CAM and Score-CAM
Abhijeet Bhattacharya
Department of Computer Science and Engineering, School of Engineering and Computer Science, Oakland University, Rochester, MI, USA
bhattacharya@oakland.edu
ABSTRACT — Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) achieve strong accuracy on image classification yet remain vulnerable to small input perturbations, raising safety concerns for real-world deployment. This project studies the robustness of a ResNet-50 classifier, transfer-trained on the 200-class Tiny ImageNet dataset, under two qualitatively different input perturbations — random Gaussian noise and optimized adversarial attacks (FGSM and PGD) — and evaluates three practical defenses: noise-augmented training, adversarial training, and test-time input denoising. We additionally compare two explainability methods, Grad-CAM and Score-CAM, to understand how a model's attention changes under perturbation. Our baseline reaches 68.1% top-1 accuracy. We find that adversarial perturbations are far more damaging than Gaussian noise of equal or larger magnitude: under heavy noise (σ=0.20) the baseline still retains 19.8% accuracy, whereas Projected Gradient Descent (PGD, ε=8/255) reduces it to 0%. Among defenses, noise augmentation nearly eliminates Gaussian degradation (52.4% at σ=0.20 vs 19.8%), and a cheap Gaussian-denoising preprocess recovers ≈23 points of accuracy under PGD. Adversarial training is the only defense that raises white-box PGD robustness above zero (13.0%), at an ≈8-point clean-accuracy cost — the canonical robustness/accuracy trade-off — and, notably, it also confers strong noise robustness (52.1% at σ=0.20) that noise augmentation does not reciprocate for adversarial attacks. A key methodological finding is that naïve adversarial training failed (it either destroyed clean accuracy through BatchNorm statistic drift, or learned no robustness and became hyper-brittle); robustness was obtained only after freezing BatchNorm, unfreezing the full backbone, and applying an epsilon curriculum. An attack-strength ablation confirms the robustness is genuine: accuracy degrades monotonically as the attack budget and iteration count grow, indicating no gradient masking. A full end-to-end 10-fold cross-validation reproduces the baseline accuracy (67.5% ± 0.6%), confirming stable, low-variance generalization, while a frozen-feature variant reaches only 48.5% ± 1.5%, showing that backbone fine-tuning contributes ≈19 points. Finally, Score-CAM produces substantially more faithful (9.0% vs 29.1% average confidence drop) and more stable (0.979 vs 0.826 correlation) explanations than Grad-CAM. The study demonstrates that robustness to random and adversarial perturbations are distinct properties requiring distinct defenses, and that explanation quality can be quantified and compared.

1. INTRODUCTION

Image classification with deep CNNs underpins applications from medical imaging to autonomous driving, domains in which a single misclassification can carry real cost. Despite their accuracy, CNNs are notoriously sensitive to perturbations of their input that are imperceptible or benign to a human observer. Understanding, measuring, and mitigating this fragility — and understanding why a model changes its decision — is therefore central to deploying deep learning responsibly.

Problem statement. Given a strong CNN image classifier, (i) how much is its accuracy degraded by random Gaussian noise versus by optimized adversarial attacks of comparable or smaller magnitude; (ii) which defense strategies are effective against each type of perturbation; and (iii) do established explainability methods (Grad-CAM, Score-CAM) differ in how faithfully and stably they explain the model's decisions?

Existing solutions. Adversarial examples were formalized by Goodfellow et al. [1], who introduced the single-step FGSM attack, and strengthened by Madry et al. [2] into the iterative PGD attack and the adversarial-training defense that remains a standard baseline. Input-transformation defenses such as denoising and smoothing offer cheaper, model-agnostic protection but can often be circumvented by adaptive attackers. On the interpretability side, Grad-CAM [3] localizes class-discriminative regions via gradients, while Score-CAM [4] removes the gradient dependence using activation-masking forward passes. A common limitation across this literature is that robustness and explainability are usually studied in isolation, and that many robustness results are reported on small datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10) at low resolution; the interaction between random-noise robustness, adversarial robustness, and explanation quality in a larger-label-space transfer-learning setting is less commonly presented together.

Proposed solution. We build a single reproducible pipeline that measures all three phenomena on one trained ResNet-50 backbone and compares defenses and explainers under identical conditions. In summary, this project makes the following contributions:

  • We provide a controlled, side-by-side comparison of random (Gaussian) versus adversarial (FGSM, PGD) perturbations on a transfer-trained ResNet-50 for 200-class Tiny ImageNet, quantifying that the two are fundamentally different threat models.
  • We implement and compare three defenses and report an asymmetry: adversarial training yields robustness to both adversarial attacks and Gaussian noise, whereas noise augmentation yields no adversarial robustness; a cheap denoising preprocess independently recovers large accuracy under PGD.
  • We document a reproducible adversarial-training recipe (frozen BatchNorm + full-backbone fine-tuning + epsilon curriculum), show through a three-attempt ablation why naïve recipes fail, and verify via an attack-strength sweep that the learned robustness is genuine (no gradient masking).
  • We quantitatively compare Grad-CAM and Score-CAM using faithfulness and stability metrics, showing Score-CAM is the more faithful and more stable explainer.

2. RELATED WORK

Goodfellow et al. (FGSM) [1]. Their problem/method: explain why neural networks are vulnerable to adversarial examples and generate them cheaply with a single gradient-sign step. Difference: we use FGSM not as an end in itself but as the weak end of a threat-model spectrum, contrasting it with iterative PGD and with non-adversarial Gaussian noise, and we evaluate defenses rather than only attacks. Why ours adds value: our setup isolates the effect of attack strength (FGSM vs PGD) and attack type (adversarial vs random) on the same model.

Madry et al. (PGD, adversarial training) [2]. Their problem/method: cast robustness as a min-max optimization and show PGD adversarial training yields models resistant to first-order attacks, primarily on MNIST/CIFAR-10. Difference: we apply PGD adversarial training to a pretrained, transfer-learned ResNet-50 at 224×224 for 200 classes — a regime where BatchNorm statistics and limited fine-tuning capacity introduce failure modes not present when training small models from scratch. Why ours adds value: we surface and solve those transfer-specific failure modes explicitly and confirm the result is not gradient masking.

Selvaraju et al. (Grad-CAM) [3] and Wang et al. (Score-CAM) [4]. Their problem/method: produce class-discriminative saliency maps — Grad-CAM via gradients, Score-CAM via gradient-free forward masking. Difference: rather than proposing a new explainer, we evaluate both with quantitative faithfulness/stability metrics on our classifier and connect explanation behavior to robustness. Why ours adds value: we give concrete numbers (average drop, increase in confidence, stability under noise) rather than only qualitative heatmaps.

3. A MOTIVATING EXAMPLE

Consider a traffic-sign or medical-image classifier deployed in the field. A camera on a car will naturally encounter random corruption — sensor noise, rain, low light — which resembles Gaussian perturbation. A malicious actor, by contrast, could craft an adversarial sticker or digital perturbation designed specifically to flip the model's decision while looking unchanged to a human. Our results make the stakes concrete: the same ResNet-50 that still classifies 19.8% of images correctly under heavy random noise is driven to 0% accuracy by a PGD perturbation bounded to only ε=8/255 — a change too small to perceive. A defender who tested only against random noise would badly overestimate the model's safety. This gap motivates measuring both perturbation types, deploying targeted defenses, and using explainability to verify that a model attends to meaningful image regions rather than brittle artifacts.

4. APPROACH

4.1 Data Collection and Preprocessing

Data sources. We use Tiny ImageNet (Stanford CS231n) [6], a 200-class subset of ImageNet: 100,000 training images (500/class) and 10,000 validation images (50/class), stored as 64×64 RGB JPEGs. It is downloaded and extracted programmatically. The training split is organized one folder per class; the validation split ships as a flat directory with an annotation file, which we parse to reconstruct a labeled class-folder layout so both splits read through an identical generator.

Preprocessing. Images are resized 64×64 → 224×224 (ResNet-50's native resolution) and scaled to [0,1]. Crucially, the ImageNet caffe-style normalization (RGB→BGR channel swap and per-channel mean subtraction) is implemented as the model's first layers, so that the image generators and every perturbation (Gaussian noise, FGSM, PGD) operate in the clean [0,1] space while the backbone still receives correctly normalized inputs. Training augmentation is horizontal flipping; the noise-augmented defense adds on-the-fly Gaussian noise (σ=0.10) to roughly half of each batch. Perturbations for evaluation are generated on the fly.

4.2 Model Selection and Training

Algorithms used. The classifier is ResNet-50 [5], initialized from ImageNet weights. We chose ResNet-50 because its residual connections make a deep network trainable and ImageNet pretraining compensates for Tiny ImageNet's small, low-resolution images — a standard, strong transfer-learning choice. Attacks are FGSM (single-step) and PGD (7-step, ε=8/255, step 2/255). Defenses are noise-augmented training, adversarial training, and test-time input denoising (Gaussian/median smoothing). Explainers are Grad-CAM and Score-CAM.

Training process. The baseline is trained in two stages: first the classification head (Adam, lr 1e-4), then the upper backbone is fine-tuned (lr 1e-5), each with best-validation checkpointing. The noise-augmented model is fine-tuned identically with noise injection. The adversarial model uses a custom training loop with a mixed clean+adversarial loss and is developed as a three-attempt iteration (Section 5.3); the final recipe freezes all BatchNorm layers (so their running statistics stay on the clean distribution), unfreezes the full backbone (for capacity), lowers dropout to 0.2, and ramps the PGD epsilon from 0 → 8/255 over the first three epochs (an epsilon curriculum), checkpointing on robust (PGD) validation accuracy with early stopping. All models are saved as weights-only checkpoints and loaded by rebuilding the architecture, avoiding serialization of the in-graph preprocessing ops.

5. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION

5.1 Methodology

Research questions / hypotheses. RQ1: adversarial perturbations degrade accuracy more than Gaussian noise of equal/greater magnitude (H1). RQ2: each perturbation type is best countered by a targeted defense (H2). RQ3: Grad-CAM and Score-CAM differ measurably in faithfulness and stability (H3).

Variables. The independent variables are model variant (baseline, noise-augmented, adversarial), perturbation type (none, Gaussian at σ∈{0.05,0.10,0.20}, FGSM, PGD), attack budget ε and iteration count, and explainer (Grad-CAM, Score-CAM). The dependent variables are top-1 accuracy and the explanation faithfulness/stability metrics. Data. All models are trained on the 100k training split and evaluated on the Tiny ImageNet validation split, whose classes and distribution match training; its 200 labels make the task non-trivial while remaining tractable on a single workstation. Because white-box PGD requires seven gradient passes per image for every model and condition, the robustness and denoising evaluations use a fixed, deterministic subset of 960 validation images (30 batches of 32, identical across all models and conditions); the attack-strength ablation uses 480 images (15 batches); and the explanation metrics use 40 images (Grad-CAM) and 15 images (Score-CAM, which needs one forward pass per activation map). The cross-validation experiments use their own held-out folds drawn from the training split (Section 5.3). Comparisons. We compare three model variants across all perturbations and two explainers against each other; the attacks are white-box (crafted against the evaluated model), the standard and strictest robustness protocol. Libraries/frameworks: TensorFlow 2 / Keras (Keras 3), Python 3.10; NumPy, SciPy (ndimage denoising), scikit-learn (stratified folds), Matplotlib, pandas, tqdm; training on 2× NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada (48 GB) GPUs.

5.2 Evaluation Metrics

  • Top-1 (and top-5) accuracy — the standard classification metric; the fraction of images whose predicted class matches the label. Used for clean, noise, and adversarial conditions because the task is balanced 200-way classification.
  • Average Drop % (↓ better) and Increase in Confidence % (↑ better) — faithfulness metrics from the CAM literature [4]: how much class confidence falls (or rises) when the input is masked by the explanation heatmap. They measure whether the highlighted region actually drives the prediction.
  • Stability correlation (↑ better) — correlation between the heatmap on a clean image and on the same image under mild noise; measures explanation robustness.

5.3 Results

Gaussian noise (RQ1, H1). Figure 1 shows the baseline degrading steeply with noise while both hardened models stay nearly flat. At σ=0.20 the baseline retains only 19.8% accuracy, versus 52.4% for the noise-augmented model and 52.1% for the adversarially trained model.

Adversarial attacks (RQ1/RQ2, H1/H2). Figure 2 is the central robustness result. FGSM and PGD at ε=8/255 drive both undefended models to near-zero (PGD = 0%), dramatically worse than any noise condition — supporting H1. The adversarially trained model resists these attacks (FGSM 19.1%, PGD 13.0%) for an ≈8-point clean-accuracy cost (68.1% → 60.1%), the classic robustness/accuracy trade-off.

Attack-strength ablation (robustness is genuine). Figure 3 sweeps the PGD budget and iteration count against the adversarial model (480-image subset). Accuracy falls monotonically as ε grows (43.5% → 7.3% from ε=2/255 to 16/255) and as the step count grows (45.4% → 11.7% from 1 to 10 steps). This graceful, monotonic degradation is the expected signature of true robustness and rules out gradient masking, in which a model only appears robust because gradients are obfuscated. The default setting (ε=8/255, 7 steps) gives 12.9% on this subset, consistent with the 13.0% measured on the 960-image evaluation subset.

Input-denoising defense (RQ2, H2). Figure 4 shows that a cheap Gaussian-blur preprocess recovers ≈23 points of accuracy from a 0% baseline under PGD, and also improves the adversarial model (13.0% → 18.5%) — a complementary, model-agnostic test-time defense.

Adversarial-training iteration (methodological finding). Figure 5 summarizes three attempts. With BatchNorm trainable (v1), its running statistics drifted onto adversarial images and clean accuracy collapsed to 3.9%. With BatchNorm frozen but only the top block trainable (v2), clean accuracy was preserved (70.8%) but the model learned no robustness and became hyper-brittle (2.7% under σ=0.05 noise). Only v3 — frozen BatchNorm, full-backbone capacity, and an epsilon curriculum — achieved the intended trade-off (60.1% clean, 13.0% PGD-robust).

Explainability (RQ3, H3). Figure 6 shows Score-CAM is markedly more faithful (9.0% vs 29.1% average confidence drop) and more stable (0.979 vs 0.826 correlation under noise) than Grad-CAM. Score-CAM also has a higher increase-in-confidence rate (33.3% vs 25.0%), but this corresponds to 5 of 15 versus 10 of 40 images and is not statistically distinguishable at these sample sizes (Fisher's exact test, p≈0.73).

Cross-validation (model stability). To verify that our comparisons rest on a stable model rather than a lucky train/validation split, we ran a full end-to-end stratified 10-fold cross-validation: for each of the ten folds a complete ResNet-50 is trained from ImageNet initialization using the same two-stage transfer recipe as the baseline (90,000 training / 10,000 held-out images per fold), and held-out top-1 accuracy is recorded. As shown in Figure 7, the ten independently trained models yield a mean accuracy of 67.5% ± 0.6% (95% CI [67.1%, 67.9%]; per-fold range 66.8%–68.6%), essentially reproducing the single-run baseline (68.1%). The very tight ±0.6% spread across ten full trainings confirms that the learning procedure generalizes reliably and that the single-run accuracies reported elsewhere are representative, not artifacts of a particular split.

Frozen-feature cross-validation (contribution of fine-tuning). As a cheap complement, we also ran a stratified 10-fold cross-validation in which the ImageNet backbone is frozen, its 2048-d features are cached once, and only the classifier head is trained per fold (pool of 10,000 training images, 50/class; 1,000 held-out images per fold). This variant reaches only 48.5% ± 1.5% (95% CI [47.4%, 49.6%]; per-fold range 46.2%–51.1%; Figure 8). Two conclusions follow. First, fine-tuning the backbone on Tiny ImageNet contributes ≈19 points of accuracy (48.5% → 67.5%), so the end-to-end training stage, not just the pretrained representation, is essential. Second, the larger spread (±1.5% vs ±0.6%) is explained by the ten-times smaller held-out folds — the binomial standard error at n=1,000 is ≈1.6%, matching the observed fold-to-fold standard deviation — rather than by any instability in the method.

Statistical significance. The robustness accuracies are each measured on the same 960 images, so the binomial standard error of a single accuracy is at most √(0.25/960) ≈ 1.6% (a 95% margin of about ±3.2 points). The headline differences are far outside this margin: Gaussian σ=0.20, baseline vs noise-augmented, 19.8% vs 52.4% (difference 32.6 points, z≈15.8); white-box PGD, baseline vs adversarial, 0.0% vs 13.0% (z≈12.0); and PGD with denoising on the baseline, 0.0% → 23.2% (z≈17.0); all p<10−30. The smaller effects are also significant: the adversarial model's clean-accuracy cost of 8.0 points (68.1% vs 60.1%, z≈3.7, p<0.001) and its 5.5-point gain from denoising (13.0% → 18.5%, z≈3.3, p<0.001). These two-proportion z-tests treat the conditions as independent samples; because every condition uses the same images, the paired differences are, if anything, more precise. Conversely, the noise-augmented and adversarial models are statistically indistinguishable under σ=0.20 noise (52.4% vs 52.1%, z≈0.1), which is why we describe adversarial training as nearly matching the dedicated noise defense there. For the explanation metrics, the large average-drop (9.0% vs 29.1%) and stability (0.979 vs 0.826) gaps are consistent across the evaluated images, but the increase-in-confidence gap is not significant (p≈0.73), and all explanation results rest on small samples (15–40 images). The full end-to-end 10-fold cross-validation provides a strong empirical complement: the across-fold standard deviation of ten independently trained models is only ±0.6%, an order of magnitude smaller than the double-digit accuracy gaps between conditions, so those gaps cannot be attributed to split-to-split or initialization variance.

5.4 Discussion

All three hypotheses are supported. H1 holds decisively: adversarial perturbations exploit the model's precise decision geometry, so a worst-case ε=8/255 step collapses accuracy to zero, whereas random noise of larger magnitude only shifts inputs diffusely and leaves 20–52% accuracy. This reflects a property of high-dimensional decision boundaries — adversarial directions are rare but devastating, random directions common but mild. H2 holds with an informative asymmetry. Adversarial training emerged as the strongest single defense: it is the only method with non-zero white-box robustness (13.0% PGD, 19.1% FGSM) and it independently acquired strong Gaussian-noise robustness (52.1% at σ=0.20), statistically indistinguishable from the dedicated noise-augmented model. The converse does not hold — noise augmentation gave zero adversarial robustness — indicating that training against worst-case perturbations subsumes robustness to random ones, but not vice versa. The iteration in Figure 5 explains the mechanism behind obtaining that robustness: frozen BatchNorm is necessary to keep normalization statistics on the clean distribution, but not sufficient — the network also needs trainable capacity (full backbone) and a learnable target (epsilon curriculum) to acquire robust features rather than merely sharpening its clean boundary. The frozen-feature cross-validation reinforces this point from the clean side: with the backbone frozen, even clean accuracy drops by ≈19 points, so trainable backbone capacity matters for both accuracy and robustness. The ablation in Figure 3 confirms the result is real robustness, not gradient masking, since stronger attacks monotonically reduce accuracy. Input denoising is a useful complement: it is cheap, model-agnostic, and recovers ≈23 points on the undefended model and ≈5.5 points even on the robust one, though as a non-adaptive defense it would weaken against an adaptive attacker. H3 is supported, with one caveat: Score-CAM's gradient-free masking yields explanations that track the model's evidence more faithfully (a much lower average drop) and remain more stable under noise, at the cost of many forward passes; its higher increase-in-confidence rate points the same way but is not significant at the sample sizes used.

6. LIMITATIONS

  • White-box robustness is modest. 13.0% PGD robustness, while non-zero and genuine, is far from the clean accuracy; adversarial training at 224×224 on 200 classes is compute-limited and would benefit from more epochs, TRADES-style losses, and hyper-parameter search.
  • Evaluation subset. Robustness and denoising accuracies are measured on a fixed 960-image subset of the validation split (480 for the ablation) because white-box PGD is expensive. This bounds each accuracy to about ±3 points (95%), ample for the headline comparisons but too coarse to resolve differences of one or two points; a full 10,000-image evaluation would tighten this about threefold.
  • Denoising is non-adaptive. The denoising defense is evaluated against a fixed (non-adaptive) PGD attacker; a stronger adaptive attacker (e.g., BPDA) that accounts for the denoiser would likely reduce its benefit.
  • Single-run defenses. The full 10-fold cross-validation establishes the stability of the baseline training procedure (±0.6%), but the noise-augmented and adversarial models were each trained once; cross-validating the adversarial-training procedure (10× adversarial trainings) was beyond the available compute, so its variance is not separately bounded.
  • Explainer scope. Faithfulness/stability metrics were computed on the baseline model only, on 40 (Grad-CAM) and 15 (Score-CAM) images; at these sizes the increase-in-confidence comparison is inconclusive, and a larger, cross-model, under-attack explanation study is future work.
  • Dataset scale. Tiny ImageNet is low-resolution (64×64 upscaled); conclusions may shift at full ImageNet resolution.

7. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

This project delivered a single, reproducible pipeline that measures random-noise robustness, adversarial robustness, three defenses, and two explainers on one transfer-trained ResNet-50. The most important points: (1) random and adversarial robustness are distinct properties — Gaussian noise leaves 20–52% accuracy where PGD leaves 0%; (2) adversarial training is the strongest single defense, uniquely providing white-box robustness while also subsuming noise robustness, whereas noise augmentation does not generalize to adversarial attacks; (3) obtaining that robustness on a pretrained network is a non-trivial engineering problem whose solution (frozen BatchNorm + full-backbone fine-tuning + epsilon curriculum) is a transferable lesson, and an attack-strength ablation confirms the robustness is genuine; (4) the training procedure is stable (67.5% ± 0.6% over ten end-to-end folds), and backbone fine-tuning accounts for ≈19 points over a frozen-feature classifier; and (5) Score-CAM provides more faithful and stable explanations than Grad-CAM.

Future work. To raise white-box robustness: train adversarially for more epochs with a TRADES-style loss and report per-seed variance. To tighten the estimates: evaluate robustness on the full 10,000-image validation set and the explanation metrics on several hundred images. To harden the denoiser: evaluate against adaptive (BPDA) attacks and combine denoising with adversarial training. For explainability: extend the faithfulness/stability comparison across all three models and under attack, to test whether robust models yield more faithful explanations. To scale: repeat on full-resolution ImageNet or a safety-critical domain such as traffic-sign recognition.
